The Original Hem: How to Keep Jeans Looking Factory-Fresh After Alteration

Mira Voss Aug 2, 2026 6 min read

There is a moment every denim lover dreads: the tailor snips the factory hem — the two parallel rows of golden chain-stitch — and replaces them with an ordinary straight stitch. It never looks quite the same again. It doesn't have to be this way.

Why the factory hem matters

The hem of a pair of quality jeans is not stitched by hand or even by a regular sewing machine. It is produced by a chain-stitch machine called a Union Special, which creates a ropelike, twisted stitch that rolls the fabric naturally as it wears. That roping effect — the subtle curl you see at the ankle of a well-worn pair — is part of what makes premium denim look so alive.

A regular alteration trims off that stitch and replaces it with a flat lockstitch. The result is a clean hem that simply doesn't behave like the original: it won't rope, it won't fade in the same way, and sharp-eyed denimheads will spot it from across the room.

"A great alteration disappears. The garment should look like it was always meant to fit this person."

— Amara Voss, Head Cutter

The original-hem technique

The good news: your factory hem can survive an alteration. The technique is called hem preservation or an original hem. Instead of cutting the fabric at the new length, the tailor:

  • marks the new break length with you wearing the shoe you'll actually pair with the jeans;
  • removes the original hem stitch threads carefully, keeping the fold intact;
  • shortens the leg from the inside, then reattaches the original hem at the new length with a strong blind stitch;
  • presses the new seam so the roped hem still rolls naturally.

The stitches that hold the original hem in place are new, but everything you can see — the chain-stitch roping, the golden thread, the wear pattern — is exactly as the factory made it.

You should ask for it

Any good tailor offers original-hem service for jeans and chinos. Price premium: roughly $6.

One caveat

On heavily faded or pre-ripped jeans, the removed section may not be reusable — a full re-hem may be kinder.

When it isn't worth saving the hem

If your jeans are tapered below the knee, shortening with an original hem can sometimes create slight fabric bunching behind the ankle. In those cases we'd suggest a simple, clean-rolled hem instead — it sits closer to the body and looks sharper in a boot cut. Honest advice is part of the job; we'll tell you which method suits your leg, your shoe and your style.

Three questions to ask your tailor

  1. "Can you do an original hem on these jeans?" — if the tailor hesitates, they likely can't.
  2. "Will the roping survive?" — a yes means they understand chain-stitch construction.
  3. "What's the cost difference?" — transparent pricing is the mark of a trustworthy shop.

At StitchCraft, original-hem denim service is second nature — we've preserved thousands of factory hems since 2013. Bring your favourite pair in, and ask for the hem that disappears.

The Relaxed Blazer: Why Looser Fits Need Even Better Tailoring

The "oversized" blazer is the most forgiving silhouette in the shop window — and the most demanding on the cutting table. A garment that fits nowhere and hangs well has been engineered that way, millimetre by millimetre.

Loose doesn't mean careless

When a jacket is meant to be trim, small errors hide behind tension: the fabric is pulled close to the body, so a sleeve that pitches a few degrees off reads as a wrinkle you can live with. In a relaxed fit there is no tension to mask anything. The shoulder hangs, the sleeve swings, the back rolls — and every proportion is visible in full.

That's why a good relaxed blazer starts with the shoulder seam pitched slightly longer than a classic, with a softer, hand-rolled pad. The sleeve head is set with more ease so the arm moves freely without dragging the lapel.

"Loose shoulders are a choice. A shoulder that sags is a mistake. The difference is the cut."

— Owen Marsh, Master Cutter

The three measurements that matter

  • Back balance — how the jacket sits at the nape. Too short and the collar stands away from your neck; too long and it bubbles at the back hem.
  • Sleeve pitch — the angle of the sleeve when your arm hangs naturally. Wrong pitch is why "relaxed" jackets bunch at the bicep.
  • Break point — where the lapel turns. Lower breaks lengthen the torso; higher breaks add shoulders. Loose fits look best with a soft, slightly lower break.

What to fix on an off-the-rack relaxed blazer

Most ready-made relaxed blazers need the sleeves shortened (they're cut long on purpose), the waist suppressed an inch even in a boxy cut, and the back balance corrected. All three are quick jobs — expect two to three days, not two weeks.

Bring the blazer you love most on the hanger but less on your body. We'll show you exactly where the drape should fall — and whether it's worth saving or swapping.

Wool Between Seasons: Brushing, Steaming & Storing

A good suit outlives ten seasonal trends. The difference between a five-year suit and a twenty-year suit isn't the cloth — it's what happens in the closet when nobody is wearing it.

Brush, don't reach for the machine

Wool sheds surface dust and crumbs if you brush it while the fibres are still relaxed — the morning after you wear it. A soft horsehair brush, ten light strokes down each sleeve and the lapels, is all most suits ever need. Dry cleaning every week, by contrast, is the fastest way to strip the bloom from a fine cloth.

Rest it, then steam it

Wool has memory and it has fatigue. After a day's wear, hang the suit and leave it for at least 24 hours so the fibres relax back to shape. For creases, use a handheld steamer held six inches away — never a hot iron directly on the cloth. The steam lifts wrinkles and refreshes the nap without pressing a shine into the shoulders.

"Clean wool doesn't attract moths. It's the sweat, food and skin oils that feed the larvae — and the closet that hides them."

— Owen Marsh, Master Cutter

Storing between seasons

  • Clean or at least steam and brush before long storage — moths target the invisible stains.
  • Use a broad, rounded wooden hanger. Wire hangers dent the shoulders permanently.
  • Cover with a breathable cotton or muslin bag. Plastic suffocates wool and traps moisture.
  • Add cedar blocks or lavender sachets — natural deterrents, refreshed yearly.
  • Give the suit room to breathe in the wardrobe; crammed rails crush the lapel roll.

When to bring it in

If the collar is greasy at the nape, the elbows have lost their sheen, or a moth has made its move before you did, don't reach for the scissors. We can recut, re-press and invisibly repair almost everything — moth damage included. Bring it in early; the longer a hole waits, the more fabric it takes with it.

Alter or Replace? A Tailor's Honest Guide

Half our consultations start with the same honest answer: don't spend the money. Here is the checklist we run through before we ever pick up the scissors — so you know when an alteration is a bargain and when it's a waste.

The three-two-one rule

As a rule of thumb, a tailor can comfortably take in a garment up to two sizes, and let out only one. Beyond that, the seams run out of room, the proportions start to fight, and the piece stops looking like the one you bought.

Where structure is the deciding vote

Simple trousers, skirts and shirts are almost always worth altering — there's little to lose and a perfect fit to gain. The calculus changes with structured garments: a suit jacket is a network of canvas, padding and interfacing. Re-cutting a jacket's shoulders or chest is a rebuild, not an alteration, and can cost more than the jacket.

"We'll tell you the truth even when it loses us the job. A returned favour is worth more than a returned jacket."

— Amara Voss, Head Cutter

The four questions to ask yourself

  1. Is the fabric worth it? — natural fibres and quality cloth reward alterations; thin synthetics rarely do.
  2. Is the garment in good shape? — pilling, faded shoulders or a blown hem suggest it's lived its life.
  3. How much needs to change? — beyond the two-one rule, walk away.
  4. What's the alteration worth vs. the replacement? — if the quote is more than half the retail price, replace it.

The honest verdict

Waists, hems, sleeves, seats and trouser hips: alter, almost always. Jacket chests, coat shoulders and any garment you plan to wear for one season only: replace. When in doubt, bring it in and let us look at it with you. The consultation is free, and so is the straight answer.

Don't Rub! The First 60 Seconds After a Stain

The first sixty seconds decide whether a stain lives or dies. Almost everything people do in that window — scrubbing, hot water, immediate washing — is exactly wrong.

The number one rule: blot, never rub

Rubbing grinds the stain deeper into the weave and crushes the fibres, pushing the pigment past the point any professional can reach. Instead, lay the garment flat and blot with a clean white cloth or paper towel, working from the outside of the stain toward the middle so it can't spread.

Your sixty-second drill

  1. Blot — lift as much as you can before touching anything wet.
  2. Cold water, inside-out — run cold (never hot) water through the back of the stain to flush it out, not set it.
  3. Neutralise — for oil and grease, dust on cornflour or talc and let it sit; it lifts, you don't press.
  4. Don't heat it — no dryer, no iron, no hot wash until the stain is gone. Heat sets almost everything permanently.

"Tell your dry cleaner what the stain is. We can't treat a mystery. The colour, the day and the dinner matter."

— Owen Marsh, Master Cutter

What your dry cleaner won't tell you

  • Delaying the drop-off is the single biggest cause of a "permanent" stain. Forty-eight hours is the safe window.
  • Point the stain out at the counter. If it isn't logged, it may not be treated.
  • Some stains (wine, tea, ink) are best treated with water-based agents; others (oil, grease) need solvent. Treating both at once can fix the stain but ruin the dye.
  • A little home detergent on delicate silk can do more damage than the stain itself. When in doubt, bring it in dry.

If a stain has already set or the fabric is delicate, don't gamble with home chemistry. Bring it in within two days and name the culprit — we'll do everything that can be done.

The Co-ord Revival: Tailored Sets, One Clean Line

Matching sets are back — but a co-ord only reads as "one clean line" when the two pieces are cut to the same cloth and the same body. That's where the tailor earns their keep.

Why a set looks different to separates

A jacket and trouser bought separately make two good pieces that argue with each other. A co-ord is a single statement that happens to be worn in two pieces. The trick is matching silhouette and proportion: the same break in the trouser, the same shoulder line in the jacket, and fabric that falls identically top and bottom.

The common faults we fix on sets

  • Mismatched lengths — trouser break and jacket hem should rhyme, not echo.
  • Sleeve and leg width — a tapered leg under a full sleeve looks unbalanced; we re-pitch both.
  • Waist suppression — the same inch taken at both waist and hip keeps the line continuous.
  • Collar and lapel roll — the set should lie flat from the nape down, in both garments.

"A co-ord is a silhouette with a hemline. Alter the two pieces together and the eye reads one shape; alter them apart and it reads two mistakes."

— Amara Voss, Head Cutter

How to make it effortless

Buy the set in your natural size and let the tailor do the rest. Shorten the trouser to the exact break you want, suppress the jacket waist a touch, and taper the sleeve so the wrist shows the same sliver of cuff as the shirt you pair with it. When both pieces are fitted to the same points, the set looks intentional rather than coordinated.

And if one piece fits and the other doesn't, don't force it — we'll often recut the jacket to match the trouser's line, or suggest mixing the trousers with another piece you own. The best co-ords are the ones that stopped looking like a costume.
